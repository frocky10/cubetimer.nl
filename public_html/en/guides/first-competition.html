Your first WCA competition

Updated 20 October 2026 · 5 min read

At an official World Cube Association (WCA) competition you get an official time, a place in the world rankings and your own WCA ID. You don't need to be fast: at almost every competition there are people who solve the cube in a minute and a half. This guide explains how to register, what happens on the day and exactly how an attempt works.

Finding a competition and registering

All official competitions are listed on worldcubeassociation.org, under Competitions. Filter by your country or region. Most countries in Europe have several a year, often in a sports hall, school or community centre.

  • Create a free account on the WCA website and register via the competition's page.
  • When you register you choose which events you'll do. Don't do too many the first time: the 3x3 plus one or two others is plenty.
  • There's almost always a registration fee, and a maximum number of competitors. Popular competitions sometimes fill up within minutes, so check on the page when registration opens.
  • Read the time limit and cutoff for each event (see below). There's no minimum level, but you do need to beat a time limit of, say, ten minutes per solve.

What to bring

  • A puzzle for every event you're registered for. It has to meet the regulations: an ordinary speedcube is fine. Stickers mustn't be peeling or damaged.
  • A pouch or bag to keep your puzzles in, with your name on it. On the day you hand in your cube to be scrambled, and it helps if it's recognisable.
  • Food and drink. A competition often lasts all day, with plenty of waiting.
  • For your first competition, some ID: new competitors usually have their name and date of birth checked. The competition page says what's needed.

How the day works

The competition page has a schedule. Each event has one or more rounds, and each round is split into groups. At the start you'll be told which group you're in.

  1. When your group is called, you hand your cube in to the scramblers. They scramble it behind a screen so you can't see it.
  2. You get a scorecard and wait until your name is called. A judge takes your cube to a table with a timer.
  3. You do five attempts (in most events). After each one the judge writes down your time and you sign the card.
  4. Then it's your turn to help: as a judge, scrambler or runner for another group. That's part of it, and it's the fastest way to really learn the rules. If you don't know how, just say so; everyone is happy to explain.

An attempt, step by step

At a competition you use a stackmat: a timer with two hand pads. It works slightly differently from a space bar.

  1. The judge puts the cube in front of you under a cover and asks if you're ready. When you say yes, they lift the cover and the fifteen-second inspection begins. You may pick up the cube and look at it, but not turn it.
  2. At eight and at twelve seconds the judge calls out the time. Put the cube down and place your hands flat on the timer.
  3. Lift your hands to start. The timer runs from that moment.
  4. When the cube is solved, put it down and place both hands flat on the timer to stop it. Don't touch the cube again until the judge has checked it.

If you start between fifteen and seventeen seconds after inspection began, you get a +2. Start any later and the attempt is a DNF. You also get a +2 if a layer is more than 45 degrees off at the end, so that one more move is needed. More than one move from solved is a DNF.

Practise this at home. In the timer, turn on WCA inspection and a hold time: then you have to hold the space bar until the lights turn green, just like on a stackmat.

Rounds, cutoffs and time limits

  • Advancing. After the first round only part of the field goes through, for example the best sixteen or the best half. At least a quarter of the competitors are always eliminated.
  • Cutoff. In some events you first get two attempts. If one of them is under the cutoff (two minutes, say), you get to do the other three as well. Otherwise your round ends after two attempts.
  • Time limit. If an attempt takes longer than the limit, the judge stops you and it's a DNF. In blindfolded events and the big cubes the limit is sometimes cumulative: it applies to all your attempts together.

How your ranking is decided is explained in Ao5, Ao12 and your stats: in most events your Ao5 counts, with your best single as the tiebreak.

Afterwards

A few days after the competition the results appear on the WCA website. You then get a WCA ID: a code with the year of your first competition, four letters of your name and a sequence number. From then on your profile there shows all your official times and your ranking in your country, your continent and the world.

Preparing with CubeTimer

Tournament mode in the timer simulates a competition: three rounds of five solves, with a cutoff and a time limit, against a field at the level you choose. You can also replay a real past WCA competition, with the real rounds, the real competitors and their times from back then, and with the real scrambles if the WCA has them. That way you see straight away where you would have finished.

Don't worry if your first round is disappointing. Almost everyone is nervous the first time and a few seconds slower than at home. That's part of it, and by your second competition it's already a lot less.
