What is a good time on the Rubik's cube?

Updated 20 October 2026 · 4 min read

"Is twenty seconds fast?" The honest answer: it depends on who you compare yourself with. For someone who has just learned to solve the cube it's incredibly fast, at a competition it puts you in the middle of the field, and the world's best finish four times in that time. Here is roughly where the boundaries are, and how long it usually takes to get there.

The levels on 3x3

Cubers talk about their level in "sub" times: sub-30 means an average under thirty seconds. They almost always mean the average (Ao5 or Ao12), not their best single.

AverageWhat it roughly means
2 to 5 minutesYou've just learned to solve the cube and are still searching at every step.
1 to 2 minutesYou know the beginner's method by heart.
Sub-1:00The beginner's method is in your fingers. The first real milestone.
Sub-30You're working on CFOP: cross on the bottom, F2L, some of the algorithms.
Sub-20A good F2L and most last-layer algorithms. A respectable time at a competition.
Sub-15You're genuinely good. Full OLL and PLL, and you look ahead during F2L.
Sub-10Years of practice. This puts you among the fastest in your country.
Under 6World class. The single world record is around three seconds.

Current records and rankings are on the website of the World Cube Association. There you can also see what the field looks like in each country.

How long does it take?

It varies enormously, but here's a rough guide for someone who practises half an hour a few times a week:

  • Sub-1:00: a few weeks after your first solve.
  • Sub-30: a few months, once you switch to CFOP.
  • Sub-20: somewhere between six months and a year and a half.
  • Sub-15: often two years or more.

Some people go much faster, and someone who practises for hours every day gets there in a fraction of the time. Age matters less than you'd think; adults who start get fast too, though often a bit more slowly than teenagers who cube every day. Which steps you learn along the way is in from beginner to CFOP.

Compare yourself with yourself

The easiest thing is to look only at your own times. Your Ao100 this month against last month says more about your progress than any leaderboard. In CubeTimer your best averages per event are in your statistics, and with an account they're saved on your profile, including your worst time.

If you do want to measure yourself against others, there are three ways:

  • The leaderboards on CubeTimer, per event and per kind of average, also for just today or this week. There's also a leaderboard with only the people you follow.
  • Tournament mode, where you play three rounds against a field at the level you choose: from beginners (around 45 seconds) to World Championship level (around six and a half seconds). Or replay a real past WCA competition, against the real times from back then.
  • A real competition. That's the only place where a time officially counts. Read your first WCA competition if you're not sure it's for you: it almost always is, even if you're not fast yet.

Being fast isn't everything

Most cubers never get sub-10, and enjoy it just as much. At a competition most of the field is well above ten seconds, and there too it's mostly about your own personal best. A new record of 35 seconds is just as much a record as one of seven.
