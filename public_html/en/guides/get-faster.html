How to get faster at cubing

Updated 20 October 2026 · 5 min read

Getting faster happens on its own at first: a few seconds off every week. Then it gets harder, and sometimes your average stays stuck for weeks. This guide is about what helps then. Almost none of it is about turning faster.

Most of your time is in the pauses

Watch a video of one of your own solves, or pay attention while you solve. You probably turn in short, fast bursts, with moments in between where you stop and search. For most cubers slower than about fifteen seconds, most of the time is in those pauses, not in the turning.

That's why the most important skill is lookahead. While you insert one F2L pair, your eyes are already looking for the pieces of the next one. That only works if your hands can do the job without you watching them.

Practise slowly

The best exercise for lookahead sounds contradictory: solve the cube very slowly, but without stopping even once. Pick a pace at which you can manage that, even if it's half your normal speed. You'll notice that you see much more, and that you already know what to do with the next pair.

Do ten or twenty of these in a row, a few times a week. It feels slow, but a solve without pauses at a calm pace is faster than one at full speed with three stops. Once the pace feels comfortable, turn a little faster.

Plan more during inspection

You get fifteen seconds to look at the cube. Use them to plan the whole cross, down to the last move. Once that always works, try to find the first F2L pair as well: where will that corner and edge be once your cross is done?

Turn on WCA inspection in the timer and you practise under the same conditions as at a competition. Start after fifteen seconds and you get a +2, just like there.

Finger tricks and turning without regrips

Fast turning comes from your fingers, not your wrist. You do a U with your right index finger pushing the top layer to the left, a U' with your left index finger. R and R' come from your wrist, but do every other move with your fingers wherever you can.

  • Regrips (changing how you hold the cube) cost time. Choose algorithms that don't need one, even if they're a move longer.
  • Cube rotations (y, x) cost even more: you have to read the cube again afterwards. Learn to solve F2L cases in the back slots too, so you don't have to rotate.
  • Turn calmly but smoothly. A cube that locks up because a layer wasn't lined up costs more than you gain with a quick move.

Algorithms: recognising matters as much as executing

Knowing an algorithm means two things: recognising the case and executing the moves. The first one is often forgotten. If you need two seconds to look at the sides for every PLL, it barely matters how fast you turn it.

Practise recognition separately. On the learn page every case has a practice button that opens the timer with exactly that case set up. That way you drill one algorithm ten times in a row, until you no longer have to search for it. What you still need to learn is in the beginner to CFOP guide.

Measure where your time goes

You can only practise in a focused way if you know what goes wrong. A few ways to find out:

  • Film a solve and play it back slowly. Count the pauses. Where do you stop: after the cross, between pairs, when recognising OLL?
  • With a smart cube, CubeTimer does it for you. After every solve you see the time and number of moves for each phase (cross, F2L, OLL, PLL), plus your turns per second. If F2L takes two thirds of your time every time, you know where to focus.
  • Compare with an example. Find a reconstruction of a solve by a strong cuber (you can find them online, with the scramble) and do the same scramble yourself. Where do you use more moves?

Watch your average, not your best time

A fast single is nice, but it doesn't say much: an easy scramble can hand you ten seconds. Whether you're really improving shows in your Ao12 and your Ao100. How those work is explained in Ao5, Ao12 and your stats.

In CubeTimer the statistics panel shows your averages for today and this week next to those of the whole session, and the graph next to the scramble shows how your times are trending. An average that slowly drops is exactly what you want to see, even if your best time hasn't improved in weeks.

When you're stuck

Every cuber spends weeks at the same average now and then. That's normal, and it usually means one part is lagging behind. What often helps:

  • Change what you practise. Spend a week on slow solves only, or crosses only, or learn five new algorithms.
  • Practise short and often. Twenty minutes a day does more than three hours on a Sunday. After a while your concentration fades, and then you're mostly practising sloppiness.
  • Warm up. The first five solves of a session are almost always slower. Don't judge yourself on them.
  • Play a game. The game modes in the timer (streak, survival, ladder, speedrun) make you stay under a target time, which trains consistency in a different way than a normal session.

And your cube?

A good speedcube helps, but less than you'd think. The difference between a ten-euro cube and a sixty-euro one is mostly in feel and setup. Read how to choose a speedcube if you're unsure; in most cases your time is better spent practising than on a new cube.
