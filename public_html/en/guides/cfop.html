From the beginner's method to CFOP

Updated 20 October 2026 · 5 min read

You can solve the cube with the beginner's method, and now you want to get faster. Sooner or later you'll end up at CFOP, the method most speedcubers use, including the world's best. This guide lists the steps in the order you'd best learn them, and what you can roughly expect from each one.

What is CFOP?

CFOP is named after its four steps: Cross, F2L (First Two Layers), OLL (Orientation of the Last Layer) and PLL (Permutation of the Last Layer). It's also called the Fridrich method, after Jessica Fridrich, who contributed a lot to it in the 1980s.

The big difference with the beginner's method: instead of solving every piece separately, layer by layer, you combine steps. In F2L you put a corner and an edge in place together. In the last layer you do in two algorithms what the beginner's method does in four or five.

Step 0: know the beginner's method well

Only start on this once you can solve the cube without a cheat sheet. If you can't yet, follow the beginner's method on the learn page first. With some practice it gets you times between one minute and a minute and a half. It hardly gets faster than that; the number of moves is simply too high.

Step 1: the cross on the bottom

In the beginner's method you often build the white cross on top, because you can see it there. For CFOP you learn to build it on the bottom. That feels awkward for the first week, but after that you can see the F2L pieces while you're doing the cross, and you don't need to flip the cube over.

  • Every cross can be solved in at most eight moves, usually six or fewer. If you regularly need more than about eight, you're doing something the long way round.
  • Use the fifteen seconds of inspection to plan the whole cross before you start. Turn on WCA inspection in the timer and you'll practise that straight away.
  • Now and then practise crosses on their own: scramble, plan, solve the cross with your eyes closed and check whether it's right.

Step 2: F2L, intuitively first

In F2L you finish the first two layers at the same time. You find a bottom-layer corner and the edge that belongs above it, pair them up in the top layer, and insert the pair into its slot in one go. You do that four times.

There are 41 basic cases, and they're all on the learn page. Don't memorise them at first. F2L is the step where understanding why a move works pays off the most. Start with the easy cases, where the corner and edge are already next to each other or form a pair in one move, and try to work out the rest yourself. Only when you keep solving a case in a clumsy way should you look up how to do it shorter.

With intuitive F2L and the old last layer you'll soon be under 45 seconds.

Step 3: the last layer in four steps

The full last layer has 57 OLLs and 21 PLLs, 78 algorithms in total. That's too many to learn in one go. That's why nearly everyone starts with 4LLL, the four-look last layer, with about sixteen algorithms:

StepWhat you doAlgorithms
OLL part 1Yellow cross on top (dot, L shape or line)2 to 3
OLL part 2Twist the yellow corners7
PLL part 1Put the corners in place2
PLL part 2Put the edges in place4

You'll already half-know many of them from the beginner's method. With 4LLL and a decent F2L most people get under thirty seconds.

Step 4: all 21 PLLs

Next you learn full PLL. There are only 21, they come up in every solve, and you recognise them by a few fixed patterns on the sides of the top layer, such as "headlights" (two matching corners side by side). Learn two or three a week, and drill them with the practice button on the learn page: it opens the timer with exactly that case set up.

Start with the PLLs you see most often and that are short: the T, U, Y and J perms.

Step 5: all 57 OLLs

Full OLL is a big job. Many cubers take months or longer, and that's fine. You already know the seven cases where the yellow cross is solved from 4LLL. Then learn the short cases and the ones you see often first, and save the "dot" cases (no yellow edge on top at all) for last: they're the longest and come up least often.

With the full last layer and a smooth F2L you'll get under twenty seconds. What makes you faster after that is in how to get faster at cubing: mostly looking ahead during F2L, and fewer pauses.

What can you expect?

A rough guide, for someone who practises a few times a week. It varies a lot from person to person:

Are there other methods?

Yes. CFOP is the best known, but not the only one that leads to the top:

  • Roux builds two 1x2x3 blocks on the sides and solves the rest with lots of M and U moves. Few moves, few algorithms, but a very different way of looking at the cube.
  • ZZ orients all edges first, after which the rest of the solve can be done with only R, U and L.
  • Petrus builds a 2x2x2 block first and expands it.

For most people CFOP is the best place to start: there's the most material about it, and the steps build on what you already know from the beginner's method.
