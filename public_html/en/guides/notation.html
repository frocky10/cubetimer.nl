Cube notation: how to read moves and algorithms

Updated 20 October 2026 · 6 min read

Look up any algorithm and you get a string of letters like R U R' U'. That is the standard notation for the Rubik's cube, and every website, video and competition uses the same one. Once you can read it, you can follow any algorithm and apply any scramble yourself. It takes about fifteen minutes to learn.

The six faces

Each letter stands for one face of the cube:

LetterNameWhich face
RRightthe right side
LLeftthe left side
UUpthe top
DDownthe bottom
FFrontthe side facing you
BBackthe side facing away from you

A letter on its own means: turn that face a quarter turn clockwise, as if you were looking straight at that face. That last part is where beginners usually go wrong. R turns the right side away from you, upwards. L is also clockwise when you look at the left side, so it moves towards you. On U the top layer moves to the left, on D the bottom layer moves to the right.

Colours don't matter. An algorithm is about positions, not colours: R is always whichever layer is on the right at that moment.

Prime and 2

  • An apostrophe (R', pronounced "R prime") is a quarter turn counter-clockwise. So R' undoes R.
  • A 2 (R2) is a half turn. The direction doesn't matter; R2 and R2' end up in the same place. Just pick whichever direction feels best in your hands.

With these twelve moves plus the half turns you can already read every 3x3 scramble. A WCA scramble consists of nothing else, for example D2 F' R2 U2 B' L2 F U2 R' D' B2 L U' F2.

How do you hold the cube for a scramble?

At an official competition every 3x3 is scrambled with white on top and green in front. Do the same at home: then you can compare your scramble with someone else's, and you notice straight away if you made a mistake. The scrambles in the CubeTimer timer assume the same orientation.

While solving, hold the cube however you like. Most people who learn CFOP solve the cross on the bottom. That's why last-layer algorithms are written for the top layer: you can see it, and it's where your fingers work most easily.

Wide moves: two layers at once

A lowercase letter, or a letter followed by a w, turns two layers at once: the face itself and the layer next to it. r and Rw mean the same thing. On a 3x3, r is the same as R plus the middle layer in the same direction.

On bigger cubes you can turn more layers at once. Then there is a number in front: 3Rw turns the three right-hand layers of a 6x6 or 7x7. That is also how they appear in the official scrambles for those events.

Middle layers: M, E and S

The middle layers have their own letters. They don't follow a face of their own, but turn along with one of the outer faces:

  • M (Middle) is the layer between L and R, and turns the same way as L.
  • E (Equator) is the layer between U and D, and turns like D.
  • S (Standing) is the layer between F and B, and turns like F.

You'll see M a lot: in PLL algorithms like the H perm (M2 U M2 U2 M2 U M2), and in the Roux method it makes up most of the last step.

Turning the whole cube: x, y and z

Sometimes you need to turn the cube in your hands without changing anything about its state. There are three letters for that:

  • x: the whole cube turns like R.
  • y: the whole cube turns like U.
  • z: the whole cube turns like F.

The prime and the 2 work here too: y' is a quarter turn the other way, x2 turns the cube upside down. In a fast solve you want as few rotations as possible, because each one costs time and you briefly lose track of the pieces. That's why good F2L solutions use a U move more often than a y.

Reading an algorithm

An algorithm is simply a sequence of moves. A few things you'll see often:

  • Brackets are only there to group moves: (R U R' U') (R' F R F'). You just do everything in order. They show which parts go together in one smooth motion.
  • Triggers are short sequences that keep coming back. R U R' U' is known as the "sexy move" and appears in dozens of algorithms. R U R' U R U2 R' is the Sune, one of the first OLLs people learn.
  • A U move at the start or end (often in brackets, or called AUF: adjust U face) lines up the top layer before or after the algorithm. It isn't really part of the algorithm, and how many you need depends on how the case is positioned.

Reversing an algorithm is easy: read it from back to front and flip the prime on every move. The inverse of R U R' is R U' R'. That's also how the practice button on the learn page builds the setup scramble for each case: the algorithm backwards.

What about the other puzzles?

The 2x2 up to the 7x7 use the same letters. The other WCA puzzles have notation of their own, which you'll mostly see in scrambles:

  • Pyraminx: U, L, R and B turn a corner with two layers; lowercase letters (u, l, r, b) turn only the tip.
  • Skewb: the same four letters, but each move turns half the puzzle around a corner.
  • Megaminx: R++ and D-- turn a large part of the puzzle two fifths of a turn; U and U' turn the top face.
  • Square-1: pairs like (1,0) say how many steps the top and bottom layer turn, and a / is a turn of the right half.
  • Clock: moves like UR3+ say which pins are up and how many hours you turn a wheel.

Practise it yourself

The quickest way to learn notation is to use it. Open the timer, take the scramble at the top and apply it move by move, with white on top and green in front. Then undo it all, from back to front. Do that a few times and you'll read a scramble as easily as a word.

After that it's time to learn real algorithms. The beginner to CFOP guide tells you in which order, and the learn page has all of them, each with a diagram calculated from the moves themselves.
